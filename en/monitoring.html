Monitoring and annotation

Watching the model in production and collecting the human labels that measure, and improve, its quality.

1 What can go wrong

A coding model in production can degrade in several ways, which the European MLOps framework sorts into a taxonomy (Tayebjee et al. 2025, ch. 4):

2 Label-free monitoring

Most predictions are never checked by a human. The first line of monitoring therefore relies on signals that need no label. With the first model, API logs were converted daily into partitioned Parquet files, queried with DuckDB, and shown in a dashboard rebuilt every night (Faria et al. 2024). The collection of production logs is currently suspended, and restoring it is part of the target protocol. The dashboard shows:

Faria, Thomas, Nathan Randriamanana, and Tom Seimandi. 2024. Retraining Strategies for an Economic Activity Classification Model. European Conference on Quality in Official Statistics (Q2024), Estoril. https://inseefrlab.github.io/codif-ape-prez/slides/estoril-paper/index.pdf.
  • query volumes and the automation rate over time, for various thresholds;
  • the distribution of the confidence index, and how it shifts between time windows (automated tests such as Kolmogorov–Smirnov or χ² are a target);
  • the distribution of predicted codes;
  • statistics on the input text (length, new vocabulary).

These indicators raise alerts. For example, a sudden drop in the automation rate, or a shift in the confidence distribution, triggers an analysis. They cannot, however, tell whether the model is right.

3 Human annotation

3.1 Label Studio campaigns

Quality can only be measured against labels produced by experts. Annotation campaigns run on Label Studio, deployed on the platform, with one project per team of experts. Scheduled jobs handle the flow of data:

  • sampling descriptions to annotate and pushing them into the annotation queues;
  • syncing completed annotations back to object storage;
  • converting them into evaluation and training sets.

Annotating one description takes about a minute. Two campaigns have been run so far:

  • a NAF rev. 2 gold standard of about 10,000 descriptions, coded blind by the Sirene quality team;
  • a NAF 2025 campaign of about 30,000 descriptions, used both to evaluate the LLM relabelling and as training data (see Data).

3.2 Why random, blind annotation matters

Low-confidence cases are recoded by hand every day, so it is tempting to treat these manual codes as an evaluation set. They are not one: they are selected on the model’s failures, and they say nothing about the cases the model codes automatically. To estimate the accuracy of automatic coding, part of the flow must be annotated at random, by experts who do not see the model’s suggestion. Otherwise their labels are anchored on the prediction they are meant to judge.

This sample, called the golden set, is the foundation of the retraining protocol. It is the only data that can be used to:

  • estimate the accuracy of automatic coding;
  • set the threshold;
  • compare two models fairly;
  • measure drift with labels.

4 Auditing with LLMs

The project also uses LLM agents to watch predictions offline. GRAAL’s MatchVerifier checks whether a (description, code) pair is consistent with the explanatory notes of the classification, and suggests an alternative when it is not. It is too slow and too costly to run on every prediction, but it can be run in batch to:

  • prioritise which cases human reviewers should look at first;
  • flag suspicious labels in the training data;
  • arbitrate disagreements between the production model and other engines.

On another classification (COICOP), the MatchVerifier found about half of the errors, with few false rejections. That makes it a good tool for prioritising human review, but not a reliable enough one to route cases on its own.
